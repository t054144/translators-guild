# seg1 notes: Arabic and factual points left unchanged

- Ch3, {{سامٌ أكلَ الكعكةَ}} / {{أكلَ سامٌ الكعكةَ}}: putting tanwīn on the foreign name “Sam” (سامٌ) looks odd. Many writers would leave it undeclined or unvowelled (سام).
- Ch3, {{العربية الفصحى التراثية}} is given for Classical Arabic. The more usual term is {{العربية الفصحى}} / {{الفصحى التراثية}} or {{العربية الكلاسيكية}}. It is acceptable, but a reviewer may want to confirm it.
- Ch3, “Formal Arabic often starts with the verb”: this is fine as a guideline. Nominal sentences are also very common in modern news Arabic, and the text does say the subject-first order is also correct.
- Ch5, {{شبر}} → “about 20 centimetres”: a span is usually given as about 22–23 cm (9 inches). “About 20 centimetres” is a loose approximation.
- Ch7, “The course materials define collocation as …”: this refers to course materials the reader of a stand-alone handbook may not have, and it has no citation. I kept it because the rules require it.
- Ch8, {{شمّر عن ساعديه}} for _pull one’s socks up_: the Arabic means “roll up one’s sleeves, get to work”. The English means “make more effort, improve”. The two are close but not exact.
- Ch8, {{الولد ولد ولو صار قاضي بلد}} is colloquial: {{قاضي}} is used where MSA would have {{قاضيَ}}. That is normal for a proverb, but it is not MSA.
- Ch8, _Business is business_ → {{تعاشروا كالإخوة وتعاملوا كالأجانب}}: this proverb means “live like brothers, deal like strangers”. It is a reasonable cultural equivalent, but it is narrower than the English.
- Ch8, {{عصفورٌ في اليد خيرٌ من عشرةٍ على الشجرة}}: this is correct as a proverb. Note that the English says “two in the bush” and the Arabic says “ten on the tree”, which makes a good example of the same meaning in a different form.
- Ch3 intro: the citation (Farghal & Shunnaq, 1999; Ghazala, 2008) stays in the intro. STYLE.md says there should be no theory names in intros, but the citation has to stay attached to its idea.
